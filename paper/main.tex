\begin{abstract}
Lemmatization maps an inflected word to its dictionary form. Tamil is
agglutinative: nouns and verbs carry long suffix chains and stems change at
suffix boundaries, which makes lemmatization essential for search, spelling and
text analysis, and makes rule-based tools brittle. We study how small and fast
a Tamil lemmatizer can be on low-power hardware while staying close to a large
model. We build a word--lemma dataset from the two Universal Dependencies Tamil
treebanks, split by lemma so that 75\% of the test pairs have lemmas never seen
in training. Across 0.5M--5M parameters, accuracy does not grow with size:
with 3,153 training pairs, data rather than capacity is the limit. Sequence-level
distillation from a 15.8M-parameter BiLSTM teacher on 9,012 Wikipedia words
helps every model; a distilled 0.5M-parameter edit-script classifier reaches
70.9\% exact match (71.4\% on unseen lemmas), within 1.9 points of the teacher
overall and 2.9 points ahead of it on unseen lemmas. Quantized to int8 it is a
538~KB model that lemmatizes a word in 0.29~ms on a Raspberry Pi~5 with 100\%
agreement with the full-precision model. A lookup table of the 50,000 most
frequent Wikipedia word forms in front of the model raises token accuracy on
frequency-weighted text from 77.8\% to 82.5\% and the speed to about 21,000
tokens per second, in 56~MB of memory. Code, data splits, models and the
scripts behind every table are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Tamil is the mother tongue of about 69 million people in India
alone~\cite{census2011} and an official language of Sri Lanka and Singapore,
yet like most of the world's languages it has few annotated resources for
language technology~\cite{joshi2020}. It is also
morphologically rich. A Tamil noun or verb takes a chain of suffixes for case,
number, person, tense, mood and discourse clitics, so that a single lemma such
as \ta{வீடு} (house) appears as \ta{வீட்டில்}, \ta{வீடுகளுக்கு} or
\ta{வீட்டிலிருந்தே}, and the stem itself changes where suffixes attach
(sandhi). Search, spell checking, text classification and corpus analysis all
need these forms reduced to their lemma.

Existing Tamil lemmatizers are either rule-based finite-state analysers, which
are precise on the words their lexicon covers but fall back to guessers
elsewhere~\cite{thamizhimorph}, or neural models built for servers and general
pipelines~\cite{stanza, tamillemmapkg}. Many uses of Tamil text, however, are
on phones, kiosks and small embedded boards: offline, private and cheap. On such
devices a model's size, latency and memory matter as much as its accuracy, and
little is known about this trade-off for Tamil.

This paper asks how small and fast a Tamil lemmatizer can be on low-power
hardware while staying close to the accuracy of a large model. Its
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a word--lemma dataset from the Universal Dependencies (UD) Tamil
  treebanks with a lemma-disjoint split, so that most of the test set measures
  generalization to lemmas never seen in training, plus 2,583 human-reviewed
  pairs for frequent Wikipedia words (section~\ref{sec:data});
\item a controlled comparison of compact character models (an attention LSTM,
  a transformer and an edit-script classifier, 0.5M--5M parameters, three seeds
  each), with sequence-level knowledge distillation and an encoder ablation
  (sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:results});
\item a benchmark of accuracy against latency, model size and memory on a
  Raspberry Pi~5 across three inference runtimes and int8 quantization, and a
  hybrid lookup + neural scheme for running text (section~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}
All numbers in this paper are produced by scripts in the released repository
from fixed seeds.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Tamil morphological analysis and lemmatization}

Early computational work on Tamil word structure targeted morphological
analysis and stemming. Anand Kumar et al.~\cite{anandkumar2010} cast Tamil
morphological analysis as sequence labelling with kernel methods, and rule-based
affix-stripping stemmers have been built for Tamil information
retrieval~\cite{thangarasu2013}. A stemmer, however, only removes affixes and
need not return a dictionary form, which a lemmatizer must. ThamizhiMorph~\cite{thamizhimorph}
is a finite-state morphological analyser and generator for Tamil built with
Foma, with lexicons for nouns, verbs and other word classes and guessers for
unknown words; it was evaluated on the UD Tamil treebank, among other data.
Neural pipelines trained on UD treebanks, such as Stanza~\cite{stanza},
include a Tamil lemmatizer trained on the Tamil Treebank, and the Tamil
dependency parser ThamizhiUDp takes its lemmas from Stanza~\cite{thamizhiudp}.
The \texttt{tamil-lemmatizer} Python package~\cite{tamillemmapkg} provides a
neural lemmatizer trained on data that its documentation does not describe.
For other Indian languages, lemmatizers have mostly been rule-based, for
example for Hindi~\cite{paul2013}. We found no published study of compact or
on-device lemmatization for Tamil or another Dravidian language.

We use the two Tamil treebanks of Universal Dependencies~\cite{nivre2020}: the
Tamil Treebank (TTB)~\cite{ttb}, a Prague-style dependency treebank converted
to UD, and the Modern Written Tamil Treebank (MWTT)~\cite{mwtt}, built from the
example sentences of a Tamil grammar. Sarveswaran~\cite{sarveswaran2024}
reviews how Tamil treebanks are built, and the recent Aalamaram
treebank~\cite{aalamaram} adds about 10,000 sentences with part-of-speech,
morphological and dependency annotation, a possible source of more training
data.

\subsection{Neural lemmatization}

Lemmatization has been modelled as classification over edit operations or edit
trees that transform a word into its lemma~\cite{morfette, lemming} and as
character-level sequence-to-sequence transduction~\cite{lematus, kanerva}.
UDPipe~2~\cite{udpipe} predicts a lemma rule per word jointly with tagging and
parsing. The SIGMORPHON 2019 shared task evaluated lemmatization in context
across 66 languages~\cite{sigmorphon2019}. Our seq2seq models follow the
attention encoder--decoder design~\cite{bahdanau} with LSTM~\cite{hochreiter} or
transformer~\cite{vaswani} layers; our edit-script classifier follows the
edit-operation line, with scripts tailored to suffixal morphology
(section~\ref{sec:edit}).

\subsection{Efficient models for devices}

Knowledge distillation trains a small student on the outputs of a larger
teacher~\cite{hinton}; sequence-level distillation trains the student on the
teacher's decoded output sequences, which suits transduction
tasks~\cite{kimrush}. Integer quantization stores weights, and optionally
activations, in 8 bits for smaller and faster inference on CPUs~\cite{jacob}.
Treviso et al.~\cite{treviso} survey these and other methods for efficient NLP.
On-device inference runtimes include ONNX Runtime~\cite{onnxruntime},
LiteRT (formerly TensorFlow Lite)~\cite{litert} and ncnn~\cite{ncnn}. To our
knowledge, no previous work measures the accuracy--efficiency trade-off of
Tamil lemmatization on edge hardware.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Gold word--lemma pairs}

We take every written word with a Tamil-script form and lemma from UD
Tamil-TTB~\cite{ttb} (CC BY-NC-SA 3.0) and Tamil-MWTT~\cite{mwtt}
(CC BY-SA 4.0), after Unicode NFC normalization, and merge duplicate pairs. For
multiword tokens the lemma comes from the first content part. The treebanks'
own train/dev/test boundaries are ignored on purpose: we split by lemma. Lemmas
are divided 80/10/10 into train, dev and test, so dev and test lemmas never
occur in training; about 5\% of the training forms are then moved to dev and
test as a ``seen-lemma'' part, so that both kinds of word are measured. This
gives 3,153 training, 668 development and 692 test pairs. 517 of the test
pairs (\emph{test unseen}) have lemmas absent from training. Stanza's Tamil
model is trained on TTB, so for comparisons with it we also report \emph{test
clean}, the 277 test words that do not occur in TTB's training section.

\subsection{Unlabelled words}

A word-frequency list from a pinned Tamil Wikipedia dump
(\texttt{tawiki-20261001-pages-articles}, CC BY-SA) counts NFC-normalized words
made only of Tamil letters, after stripping wiki markup. It supplies unlabelled
words for distillation (section~\ref{sec:kd}) and the frequent forms for the
lookup tables (section~\ref{sec:hybrid}).

\subsection{Reviewed expansion}
\label{sec:expansion}

To test whether more gold data helps, we labelled the 3,000 most frequent
Wikipedia words that occur in none of train, dev or test. For each word, four
systems proposed a lemma: ThamizhiMorph, the teacher and the two distilled
students of section~\ref{sec:kd}; the majority vote was shown as a suggestion,
with the row marked \emph{agree} (4/4), \emph{majority} (3/4) or
\emph{conflict}. Before the review, we measured on dev how often the suggestion
is right: 93.7\% for agree rows, 79.8\% for majority rows and 49.4\% for
conflict rows (76.8\% overall, against 73.9\% for the best single source). One
annotator (the author) reviewed all rows: 2,830 were accepted, 413 of them
with a corrected lemma; 39 were rejected and 131 left undecided. The suggestion
was kept for 98.7\% of agree, 83.0\% of majority and 51.6\% of conflict rows,
close to the dev estimate. After dropping pairs whose lemma is a dev or test
lemma, 2,583 pairs extend the training set to 5,736. The reviewed words cover
22.7\% of the running tokens of Tamil Wikipedia. To measure the consistency of the labels, the annotator re-lemmatized a sample
of 200 of the accepted words some days later, drawn in proportion to the three
groups, without seeing the first annotation or the suggestions; the two passes
agree on \todo{X}\% of the words (\todo{per group}). This measures
intra-annotator consistency, not agreement between annotators.

\section{Models and methods}
\label{sec:method}

All models read Unicode codepoints after NFC normalization, for words up to 48
codepoints (the 99.9th percentile of the data), and are implemented in PyTorch.
Each model is defined by a parameter budget (0.5M, 1M, 2M or 5M); widths are
scaled to meet it.

\subsection{Sequence-to-sequence models}

The \emph{LSTM} model has a character BiLSTM encoder~\cite{hochreiter} and an
LSTM decoder with attention~\cite{bahdanau}. The \emph{transformer} is a
pre-norm character encoder--decoder~\cite{vaswani} with learned positions and
two layers. Both decode greedily on the device. For export, each model is split
into an \texttt{encode} graph and a \texttt{decode\_step} graph, with the
decoding loop in host code. The BiLSTM's backward direction reads each word
reversed in place, so the encoder output does not depend on padding and
fixed-length exported graphs reproduce training exactly. Masks are applied
arithmetically and the in-place reversal is a multiplication by a permutation
matrix, so that the graphs use only operators every runtime supports.

\subsection{Edit-script classifier}
\label{sec:edit}

Tamil inflection is almost entirely suffixal, so most word--lemma pairs differ
by a suffix rewrite. From each training pair we extract an edit script: the
string removed from the end of the word and the string appended, plus, rarely,
the same at the start. For example, \ta{மரங்கள்} $\rightarrow$ \ta{மரம்}
removes \ta{ங்கள்} and appends \ta{ம்}. Removed strings are stored literally, so
a script only applies to words that end with them; scripts that do not apply to
a word are masked out in training and inference. A character CNN encoder, with
positions counted from the end of the word, predicts one script per word. A
word costs one forward pass and no decoder. The training data yields 703
scripts (1,093 with the distillation data), which cover 90\% of the test
pairs; this bounds the classifier's accuracy.

\subsection{Teacher and distillation}
\label{sec:kd}

The teacher is a 15.8M-parameter BiLSTM attention model (embedding 256, hidden
512, two layers) trained with label smoothing 0.1 and decoded with beam 4. For
sequence-level distillation~\cite{kimrush}, it labels the 20,000 most frequent
Wikipedia words. Three filters keep the test set honest and the labels clean:
words that occur in dev or test are excluded; words whose predicted lemma is a
dev or test lemma are dropped, so unseen lemmas stay unseen; and predictions
with a per-token probability below 0.9 are dropped (on dev this keeps 48\% of
the words at 90\% precision). The 9,012 remaining words are added to the gold
training pairs, and students are trained on the union. For the edit classifier
the teacher's lemma is converted to an edit script.

\subsection{Export, runtimes and quantization}

Trained models are exported to ONNX (run with ONNX Runtime~\cite{onnxruntime}),
LiteRT~\cite{litert} and ncnn~\cite{ncnn}. ONNX graphs take a dynamic batch;
LiteRT graphs are exported at fixed batch sizes 1 and 64, and ncnn graphs at
batch 1. Every export stores PyTorch's predictions for the test words, so a
device reports word-level agreement with PyTorch without needing PyTorch.

We apply post-training int8 quantization~\cite{jacob} to the exported graphs:
dynamic quantization (int8 weights, activations quantized at each call) for
ONNX Runtime and LiteRT, and static quantization with quantize--dequantize
nodes for ONNX Runtime, calibrated on the activations seen while lemmatizing
training words. Static quantization is limited to convolution and matrix
multiplication, so the additive masks stay in floating point.

\subsection{Hybrid lookup}
\label{sec:hybrid}

Word frequencies in running text are highly skewed, so a small table of
frequent forms can answer most tokens. A table maps the $N$ most frequent
Wikipedia forms to a lemma: the gold training label if there is one, otherwise
the majority vote of four systems (ThamizhiMorph, the teacher and the two
distilled students), computed once, offline. On the device, a lookup costs one
dictionary access and only misses reach the neural model. Misses are processed
in batches; for runtimes with fixed batch sizes, the host times one call of each
graph at start-up and splits each batch of misses into the cheapest
combination of graph calls.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Training and evaluation}

Models are trained with AdamW (learning rate $2\times10^{-3}$, weight decay
0.01, batch 32, gradient clipping at 1.0), with early stopping on dev accuracy
(patience 12, at most 80 epochs); the transformer uses warm-up, label smoothing
0.1 and a longer schedule. Each configuration is trained with seeds 13, 14 and
15, and we report the mean and standard deviation of exact-match accuracy. For
deployment we use seed 13 of each distilled 0.5M model.

The baselines are: the identity function (copy the word); a suffix stripper
with rules for common Tamil suffixes; ThamizhiMorph~\cite{thamizhimorph},
taking the first analysis of the first analyser in a fixed order and its
guessers otherwise; Stanza~1.15~\cite{stanza}; and
\texttt{tamil-lemmatizer}~0.0.2~\cite{tamillemmapkg}. All are run as external
tools on the same words.

\subsection{Devices and benchmark protocol}

The edge device is a Raspberry Pi~5 (four Cortex-A76 cores at 2.4~GHz, 8~GB
RAM, Debian 13, \texttt{performance} CPU governor, active cooling, no
throttling recorded). The reference is a desktop with an AMD Ryzen~9 9900X
(12 cores, 24 threads). Runtimes: ONNX Runtime 1.31, LiteRT 2.3 (2.2 on the
desktop) and ncnn 1.0.20260526; the device needs no PyTorch.

Each configuration (model, precision, runtime, threads, batch) runs in a fresh
process on the 692 test words repeated to 10,000 in a fixed order, after 200
unmeasured warm-up words; it is repeated five times and the median is
reported. We measure with one thread and with all cores, and with batch 1
(interactive use) and 64 (bulk processing; latency per word is the batch time
divided by 64). We also record peak resident memory (RSS), model size on disk
and cold start (process start to first lemma). For the hybrid runs, each test
word is repeated by its frequency in the UD corpus and the stream shuffled with
a fixed seed, as a stand-in for running text; we report token accuracy and mean
time per token.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\input{figures/out/tab_accuracy}

\subsection{Accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} compares the baselines, the teachers and the compact
models. Copying the word is right for 26.7\% of test pairs, and simple suffix
rules reach 38.7\%. ThamizhiMorph reaches 55.4\%: it is 87--88\% accurate on
the roughly 40\% of words its lexicon covers and 29--35\% on the rest, which
go to its guessers. Stanza's 84.1\% is inflated by overlap with its training
data: it scores about 96\% on test words that occur in TTB's training section
and 65.3\% on test clean. On test clean, \texttt{tamil-lemmatizer} leads with
82.7\%, ahead of our distilled LSTM (72.9\%), distilled edit classifier
(70.8\%) and teacher (69.3\%, all seed 13); its training data is
undocumented, so we cannot tell how much of test clean it has seen.

Trained from scratch, the 0.5M LSTM (70.5\%) is within 2.3 points of the
15.8M teacher (72.8\%). The edit classifier is 5.7 points behind the LSTM
overall but only 0.5 behind on unseen lemmas. The transformer memorizes
training lemmas but generalizes poorly at this data size (55.6\% on unseen
lemmas), and the 7.4M transformer teacher is worse still. With distillation,
the 0.5M LSTM (73.4\%) matches the teacher, and the 0.5M edit classifier is
the best of our models on unseen lemmas (71.4\%).

\subsection{Model size}

Figure~\ref{fig:params} and table~\ref{tab:sweep} show accuracy across the four
budgets. For every family accuracy is flat, or falls, from 0.5M to 5M
parameters: the largest LSTM is 0.7 points below the smallest, and the
transformer loses 3.6 points at 5M. With 3,153 training pairs, data and not
capacity limits accuracy, and the smallest budget is the right one to deploy.

\input{figures/out/tab_ablation}

\subsection{Encoder ablation}

The edit classifier differs from the LSTM in two ways: a CNN encoder instead of
a BiLSTM, and a single classification instead of a decoder. To separate them,
we trained a CNN encoder with the LSTM decoder at 1M parameters
(table~\ref{tab:ablation}). It loses 1.9 points to the BiLSTM on test, and 2.0
on unseen lemmas, where the edit classifier gains 1.1. Its speed gain depends
on the runtime: on the Pi with one thread, it recovers about 80\% of the gap
between the LSTM and the edit classifier on LiteRT, 40\% on ONNX Runtime and
none on ncnn. The single-pass output, not the CNN encoder, explains both the
edit classifier's speed on every runtime and its accuracy on unseen lemmas.

\subsection{Distillation}

Distillation improves every family at every size (table~\ref{tab:sweep}). The
LSTM gains 0.8--2.9 points on test and 1.5--4.7 on unseen lemmas; the edit
classifier gains 6.1--7.7 on test and 5.9--7.0 on unseen lemmas, and its gain
does not shrink with size. The edit classifier gains most because the
teacher-labelled words add 390 edit scripts and many more examples of the
rarer ones. The error analysis (section~\ref{sec:errors}) shows which errors
disappear.

\input{figures/out/tab_expansion}

\subsection{Human-reviewed expansion}

Table~\ref{tab:expansion} adds the 2,583 reviewed pairs, 82\% more gold data,
to the training set. The edit classifier gains 2.8 points on dev and 2.0 on
test from scratch, and 1.0 and 0.8 with distillation; the LSTM gains on dev
(0.7 and 1.0) but not on test. Only the edit classifier's dev gain from
scratch clearly exceeds the variation between seeds. The reviewed words are
frequent Wikipedia forms, the test words rarer treebank forms, so this data
helps the models less than its size suggests. Its main use is the lookup table
(section~\ref{sec:hybrid-results}), where it replaces voted labels with human
ones.

\subsection{Speed, size and quantization}

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} plots accuracy against latency for every exported model
on both devices, and table~\ref{tab:quant} details the two deployed models.
The edit classifier is 3--6 times faster than the LSTM at the same budget,
because it needs one forward pass instead of one decoder step per output
character. On the Pi, LiteRT with dynamic int8 is the fastest variant of both:
0.29~ms per word for the edit classifier (four threads) and 1.87~ms for the
LSTM (one thread), with 100\% and 99.9\% agreement with the fp32 model and at
most 0.2 points of accuracy lost. Quantization shrinks the models 3.6 and 2.8
times, to 538 and 796~KB. The Pareto front on the Pi runs from the int8 edit
classifier (0.29~ms, 71.0\%) to the int8 LSTM (1.87~ms, 72.5\%).

The effect of quantization depends on the platform. Dynamic int8 in ONNX
Runtime speeds up the edit classifier on the Pi (1.04 to 0.62~ms with one
thread) but slows it down three times on the x86 desktop; static int8 helps on
both. More threads help on the Pi's four cores but not on the desktop, where
the models are too small to use 24 threads. ncnn uses the least memory (40~MB
against 55--135~MB) and starts fastest (about 150~ms against up to 220~ms) but
is slower than the best runtime for these models; its fp16 mode halves the
model file but speeds up inference by at most 11\% on the Cortex-A76.

\input{figures/out/tab_hybrid}

\subsection{Hybrid lookup}
\label{sec:hybrid-results}

Table~\ref{tab:hybrid} puts lookup tables of increasing size in front of the
distilled models. A 1,000-word table (45~KB) already answers 38\% of the test
tokens, and 50,000 words (2.8~MB) answer 84\%; on Tamil Wikipedia itself the
same tables cover 40\% and 82\% of tokens. Table answers are more accurate than
the models on those tokens (86\% on test hits), so token accuracy rises from
77.8\% to 82.5\% for the edit classifier and from 78.3\% to 83.2\% for the
LSTM; on dev the edit classifier rises from 65.3\% to 74.2\%. At the same time
the mean time per token on the Pi falls six-fold, to 0.048~ms for the int8 edit
classifier, about 21,000 tokens per second, and memory stays at 56~MB. Cheap
table lookups make the slower but more accurate LSTM practical as well:
0.29~ms per token in int8 with the 50,000-word table.

\subsection{Error analysis}
\label{sec:errors}

We sorted the test errors of five models into six categories with rules over
Tamil graphemes (table~\ref{tab:errors}); table~\ref{tab:error-examples} shows
examples. The commonest errors leave the inflected form unchanged, or miss the
stem-final letter that sandhi alters (\ta{வாழவும்} $\rightarrow$ \ta{வாழு}
instead of \ta{வாழ்}). Distillation removes nearly a third of the edit
classifier's unchanged forms (89 to 62) and some of its stem-final errors (50
to 42). About 40 errors per model are words the treebank keeps as they are,
such as adverbs, postpositions and numerals (\ta{முப்பது}, thirty), which the
models over-reduce. 69 of the distilled edit classifier's 201 errors need an
edit script outside its vocabulary, a hard limit the LSTM does not have. 86
test pairs are wrong for all five models; some of them look like annotation
noise (\ta{பங்கேற்க} $\rightarrow$ gold \ta{பங்கேல்}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Recommendations}

For interactive use on a small device, the distilled edit classifier in int8
is the clear choice: 0.29~ms per word, 538~KB and the best accuracy on unseen
lemmas. Where accuracy on known lemmas matters more than speed, the distilled
LSTM is 1.7 points more accurate overall at about six times the cost. For
running text, a frequency table in front of either model is the largest single
gain in both accuracy and speed; a 5,000--10,000-word table (0.25--0.5~MB)
already gives more than half of the accuracy gain. Because model size does not
buy accuracy here, more and more varied training data, distilled or
human-labelled, is the most promising direction.

\subsection{Limitations}

The gold data is small, 3,153 training pairs from two treebanks, so all models
are data-limited and the test set follows the treebanks' conventions, for
example keeping many adverbs and postpositions unreduced. The hybrid
evaluation weights test words by treebank frequency, a stand-in for running
text from another domain. We benchmark one ARM device. The Pi~5's Cortex-A76
cores are common in mid-range phones, but Android runtime builds were not
measured, and low-end phones such as the Samsung Galaxy A13 still ship 32-bit
Android, for which the Python packages of these runtimes have no builds;
reaching them needs native runtime builds. The reviewed expansion was annotated by one
person; we report the consistency of a repeated annotation, not agreement
between annotators. We do not use sentence context, which would resolve ambiguous forms.

\section{Conclusions}

A 0.5M-parameter edit-script classifier, distilled from a 15.8M-parameter
teacher and quantized to int8, lemmatizes Tamil in 0.29~ms per word in 538~KB
on a Raspberry Pi~5, within two points of the teacher overall and ahead of it
on lemmas never seen in training. With 3k training pairs, accuracy does not
improve from 0.5M to 5M parameters, while distillation adds 6--8 points to the
edit classifier. With a 50,000-word lookup table in front, it processes about
21,000 tokens per second at 82.5\% token accuracy in 56~MB of memory. These
results show that accurate Tamil lemmatization fits comfortably on low-power
hardware, and that data, not model size, is what limits it today.
